Colorblindness comes in different types, and it causes certain colors to appear differently to
different people. A color you may see just fine may be hard to see for other people.

Deuteranomaly is the most common form of colorblindness, found in about 5% of people.
It causes Reds, Greens, and Yellows to appear similar.
Many people live with mild deuteranomaly, causing vivid autumn leaves to appear slightly dulled, 
or shades of green and orange to blend into one another.

Protoanomaly is another common form of colorblindness, seen in 1.03% of people.
Reds appear darker and duller, with oranges and yellows looking very similar to them.
Because it dims the reds in our daily lives, red lights, brakes, and danger text can be hard to spot.

Tritanomaly is a rare case of colorblindness, only held by 0.001% of people.
Commonly known as blue-yellow colorblindness, it causes blues and greens to blend together, 
and yellows to look pinkish.

Deuteranopia is a harsher form of Deuteranomaly. Rather than it being hard to perceive green, 
people with deuteranopia can't perceive green at all.
Seen in about 1% of people, Deuteranopia causes greens to be turned into shades of brownish-yellow.

Protanopia is a more extreme version of Protanomaly. Rather than it being hard to perceive red, 
people with protanopia can't perceive red at all.
Seen in about 1% of people, Protanopia makes it hard for people to differntiate 
reds, greens, and everything in between.

Tritanopia is a very rare form of colorblindness, only present in about 1 in 30,000 people.
People lose the ability to differentiate blues and greens, and yellows will always appear and light pink or grey.

Monochromacy is the rarest form of colorblindness. It has quite a few forms.
Some people can only see reds, greens, blues, or no color at all! This is known as Achromatopsia.

This program is able to discern all seven of the colorblindness types listed above.
